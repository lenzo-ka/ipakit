# A reading list

A reading list, not a bibliography. Every entry says what to read the work *for*; a work that could not earn that sentence is not here.

It is deliberately **not** the union of what the assessments in [design/](design/) rest on. Those source lists live with their documents and serve an argument — they record what each claim was checked against, and they are complete for that purpose. This page serves a reader instead. It drops sources that were consulted and decided nothing, it merges several works into one entry where one annotation covers them, and it keeps works no assessment cites at all. Nothing here is load-bearing for any claim made elsewhere in this repository, which is the point: `scripts/docquotes.py` checks quotations against sibling documents and leaves citations to books, handouts and URLs alone, so a bibliography kept for its own sake is exactly the kind of page that goes stale unwatched.

Who it is for: people building speech technology — TTS front ends, grapheme-to-phoneme, pronunciation lexicons, prosody; people working on low-resource and Indigenous language technology; people teaching this material; and people working on articulation. Where a work bears on a page in this repository, the entry says which page. Where it exposes something the repository does not do, it says that too.

## The marks

**Open access** — free at the URL given, by the publisher's, the author's or a repository's own intent.

**Author copy** — the author posts it themselves. The copy may not be the published typesetting, so page numbers taken from it are not necessarily the journal's.

**Paywalled** — no free copy was found by the routes recorded in [What was tried and not obtained](#what-was-tried-and-not-obtained).

Access status is on every entry because for a class it is the deciding fact: what students can reach for free is what can be assigned. That is curation, not metadata.

Every URL below was reached on 2 August 2026. Nothing was taken from a shadow library, and no paywall or bot check was worked around — a bot check is an access control. Several works are absent for exactly that reason, and the closing section records the attempt and its date rather than declaring the work unavailable.

## Start here

Five works, one per area, for someone new to the whole of it.

- Kaplan & Kay 1994 for why a rule cascade is a machine you can reason about.
- Browman & Goldstein 1992 for what a gesture is and why a tract has named variables.
- *Essentials of Linguistics* 2e for the phonetics and phonology underneath all of it, complete and openly licensed.
- Black & Lenzo, *Building Synthetic Voices*, for what a speech system actually asks a phonetic representation to do.
- The IPA chart and the IPA number chart, because every other entry assumes you have them open.

## 1. The rewrite calculus and its formal ground

The tradition that says what an ordered rule cascade can and cannot compute. Almost all of it is free and permanent at the ACL Anthology.

- Kaplan & Kay. 1994. "Regular Models of Phonological Rule Systems." *Computational Linguistics* 20(3), 331–378. <https://aclanthology.org/J94-3001/>. **Open access.** The load-bearing paper of the area, and the first thing to read in it. Ordered rewrite rules describe a regular relation provided a rule is not allowed to rewrite its own output; §4 names the rewritten portion the *center* of the rule and gives three application strategies — left-to-right, right-to-left, and locating every site in the original string before rewriting any of them. `ipakit`'s engine matches against a snapshot, so it is the third. §6 shows unrestricted cyclic reapplication leaving the regular world on a three-symbol example, and §7 proves ordered rewriting and two-level constraint grammars equivalent in expressive power. Bears on [calculus.md](calculus.md) and [rules.md](rules.md).
- Karttunen. 1995. "The Replace Operator." *ACL 1995*, 16–23. <https://aclanthology.org/P95-1003/>. **Open access.** Rewrite rules as an algebraic operator on regular relations, with optional replacement defined by making the identity an alternative to the replacement. That is the closest published statement of what the optional arrow in [calculus.md](calculus.md) is. Read §3.1 for the observation that starting from obligatory replacement and deriving the optional version is the easy direction, and that Kaplan & Kay go the other way.
- Karttunen. 1996. "Directed Replacement." *ACL 1996*, 108–115. <https://aclanthology.org/P96-1015.pdf>. **Open access.** Longest-match and leftmost-first semantics, which is what an implementer reaches for when a rule is ambiguous about where to fire. It is also the obvious next citation for anyone reasoning about optionality and the wrong one, because the directed operator is unconditional.
- Mohri & Sproat. 1996. "An Efficient Compiler for Weighted Rewrite Rules." *ACL 1996*. <https://aclanthology.org/P96-1031/>. **Open access.** How rewrite rules actually get compiled, with a smaller cascade than the 1994 construction and a stated finiteness condition. This is the paper a production text-normalization or g2p front end ends up implementing.
- Mohri. 1997. "Finite-State Transducers in Language and Speech Processing." *Computational Linguistics* 23(2), 269–311. <https://aclanthology.org/J97-2003.pdf>. **Open access.** The survey: composition, determinization and minimization for transducers, with the conditions under which each is available. Read it before reading any implementation.
- Mohri, Pereira & Riley. 2002. "Weighted Finite-State Transducers in Speech Recognition." *Computer Speech and Language* 16(1), 69–88. <https://cs.nyu.edu/~mohri/pub/csl01.pdf>. **Author copy.** The cascade a recognizer is built out of — grammar, lexicon, context dependency, acoustic model, composed and optimized as one machine. The copy is headed as a submission rather than the published article, so journal page numbers should not be quoted from it.
- Allauzen, Riley, Schalkwyk, Skut & Mohri. 2007. "OpenFst: A General and Efficient Weighted Finite-State Transducer Library." *CIAA 2007*, 11–23. <https://www.openfst.org/twiki/pub/FST/FstBackground/ciaa.pdf>. **Open access**, on the project's own site. The library everything above ends up running on.
- Gorman. 2016. "Pynini: A Python library for weighted finite-state grammar compilation." *SIGFSM 2016*, 75–80. <https://aclanthology.org/W16-2409.pdf>. **Open access.** The same tradition reachable from Python, which is the shortest path from reading the theory to running it.
- Hulden. 2009. "Foma: a finite-state compiler and library." *EACL 2009 Demonstrations*, 29–32. <https://aclanthology.org/E09-2008.pdf>, and *Finite-State Machine Construction Methods and Algorithms for Phonology and Morphology*, PhD dissertation, University of Arizona, <https://repository.arizona.edu/handle/10150/196112>. **Both open access.** An open compiler you can install, with the algorithms written out at length in the dissertation. Together they are the free substitute for the standard finite-state morphology textbook, which is paywalled and whose companion site is dead.
- Koskenniemi. 1983. *Two-Level Morphology: A General Computational Model for Word-Form Recognition and Production*. University of Helsinki. <http://hdl.handle.net/10138/305218>. **Open access.** The parallel-constraint alternative to a rule cascade: no intermediate representations passed from rule to rule, constraints intersected instead. Read it against Kaplan & Kay §7, which proves the two formalisms equivalent in power and says that empirical coverage cannot choose between them. The scan has no text layer, so it cannot be searched — read it, do not grep it.
- Karttunen & Beesley. 2005. "Twenty-five years of finite-state morphology." In *Inquiries into Words, Constraints and Contexts*, 71–83. <https://helda.helsinki.fi/handle/10138/33783>. **Open access.** Thirteen pages of history covering how the field got from two-level rules to replace operators. The cheapest orientation available, and free where the book-length treatment is not.
- Gerdemann & van Noord. 1999. "Transducers from Rewrite Rules with Backreferences." *EACL 1999*, 126–133. <https://aclanthology.org/E99-1017.pdf>. **Open access.** What it costs to let the right of a rule name something the left matched. Bears directly on [design/captures.md](design/captures.md), which reaches the same split between naming a matched term and covering a span of them.
- Chandlee & Heinz. 2018. "Strict Locality and Phonological Maps." *Linguistic Inquiry* 49(1), 23–60. <https://www.jeffreyheinz.net/papers/Chandlee-Heinz-2018-SLPM.pdf>. **Author copy.** Most phonological processes are input strictly local — computable with a bounded window — which is a much tighter claim than regularity and a much more useful one for anyone building a learner. Chandlee's 2014 dissertation, *Strictly Local Phonological Processes*, <https://www.jeffreyheinz.net/advisees/2014_JaneChandlee_dissertation.pdf>, **open access**, is the long version with the process-by-process survey.
- Chandlee & Heinz. 2012. "Bounded copying is subsequential: Implications for metathesis and reduplication." *SIGMORPHON 2012*, 42–51. <https://aclanthology.org/W12-2306.pdf>. **Open access.** The formal result behind the two processes a segment-rewriting notation always struggles with. Read it beside [design/captures.md](design/captures.md), which refuses the span and adopts the reference.
- Heinz & Lai. 2013. "Vowel Harmony and Subsequentiality." *MOL 13*. <https://aclanthology.org/W13-3006/>. **Open access.** Harmony is subsequential under the directional theories, and their Theorem 4 is the half worth carrying away: Majority Rules is not regular. "Harmony is finite-state" is true of the theories people actually write and false of some logically possible alternatives.
- Terlutte & Simplot. 2000. "Iteration of rational transductions." *RAIRO – Theoretical Informatics and Applications* 34(2). <https://www.numdam.org/item/ITA_2000__34_2_99_0/>. **Open access.** Rational transductions are closed under composition and not under iteration. That single fact is why a repeat-until-fixpoint block is a different kind of object from a cascade, and it is the formal ground under [design/conlang.md](design/conlang.md)'s refusal of propagation. The scan's OCR keeps the French accent in *itération* throughout, so search for both spellings.
- Bird & Ellison. 1994. "One-Level Phonology: Autosegmental Representations and Rules as Finite Automata." *Computational Linguistics* 20(1), 55–90. <https://aclanthology.org/J94-1003.pdf>. **Open access.** Autosegmental structure without derivation: tiers as constraints on one level rather than as objects a rule rewrites. It is the reason [design/tiers.md](design/tiers.md) lets a rule read a tier and not rewrite one — that restriction is where the finite-state tradition itself stops, not a caution.
- Karttunen. 1998. "The Proper Treatment of Optimality in Computational Phonology." *FSMNLP 1998*. <https://arxiv.org/pdf/cmp-lg/9804002>. **Open access.** Optimality Theory recast as lenient composition over transducers — the most readable entry to whether a constraint grammar can be compiled the way a rule grammar can.
- Frank & Satta. 1998. "Optimality Theory and the Generative Complexity of Constraint Violability." *Computational Linguistics* 24(2), 307–315. <https://aclanthology.org/J98-2006.pdf>. **Open access.** The negative half: violable constraints stay within the regular relations only when violation counting is bounded. Read it directly after Karttunen 1998, which is optimistic in exactly the places this is not.
- Gorman & Sproat. 2021. *Finite-State Text Processing*. Synthesis Lectures on Human Language Technologies 50. <https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-02179-4>. **Paywalled**; the front matter is free and establishes the chapter structure. The current textbook treatment of this material for people building systems rather than proving theorems. Named here because a reader should know it exists before deciding the field's textbooks are all from the 1990s.

## 2. Phonological theory, notation and features

- Chomsky & Halle. 1968. *The Sound Pattern of English*. Harper & Row. <https://www.fulcrum.org/concern/monographs/2z10wq58b>. **Open access**, on Fulcrum from University of Michigan Press. Worth stating plainly because it surprises people: the book the whole rule notation comes from is free and complete. Read chapter 8's formalism and the expansion conventions; that is what [rules.md](rules.md) and [design/braces.md](design/braces.md) are arguing with. Text extracted from the scan varies with the extraction settings — two runs over the same PDF disagree in places — so read rule displays off the page rather than out of a text dump.
- Zuraw & Martin, *SPE rule notation review*, UCLA Ling 200A, Fall 2004, <https://linguistics.ucla.edu/people/zuraw/200A_2004/0203RuleNotation.pdf>, and Zuraw, *Class 3: Expansion conventions*, UCLA Ling 200A, 2020, <https://linguistics.ucla.edu/people/zuraw/200A_2020/2020_03_ExpansionConventions.pdf>. **Both open access** on the course pages. They teach the notation faster than the book does, and they are best read as a pair: the earlier handout states disjunctive ordering for schemata in general, and the later one corrects that in a footnote. The pair is the evidence that the conjunctive reading of braces is a live trap rather than a settled point, which is the finding [design/braces.md](design/braces.md) turns on.
- Hayes. 2009. *Introductory Phonology*. Wiley-Blackwell. **Paywalled.** The clearest single teaching text on rules, features and derivations, and the one to want if the budget exists. Copies circulate on course sites; they are not the publisher's. Whatever copy you have, its text layer uses a custom-encoded font that mangles IPA, so transcriptions and rule statements have to be read off the rendered page — prose and page numbers extract correctly, and everything phonetic does not.
- Odden. 2005. *Introducing Phonology*. Cambridge University Press. **Paywalled.** The alternative to Hayes, with more problem sets and a stronger emphasis on doing the analysis. Same caution about circulating copies.
- Kenstowicz & Kisseberth. 1979. *Generative Phonology: Description and Theory*. Academic Press. **Paywalled**, and not obtainable by any open route tried — the copy read here was supplied directly. Its notation chapter is the one that states, in as many words, that a change in relative ordering cannot be expressed by the ordinary rule format, and that is the claim [design/captures.md](design/captures.md) is downstream of. If you can reach a copy, its treatment of transformational notation for phonology is still the fullest.
- Dresher. 2004. "Chomsky and Halle's Revolution in Phonology." University of Toronto. **Author copy.** What actually changed in 1968 and what was already there. The right thing to hand someone who has been told *SPE* invented everything.
- Goldsmith & Laks. "Generative phonology: its origins, its principles, and its successors." **Author copy.** The longer history, carrying through to autosegmental and metrical phonology. Read after Dresher if you want the line from *SPE* to what people write now.
- Ladefoged. "Features and parameters for different purposes," and "Representing linguistic phonetic structure." <http://www.linguistics.ucla.edu/people/ladefoge/PLfeaturesParameters.pdf>. **Author copy.** The argument that a feature set is chosen for a purpose and that no one set serves them all — descriptive adequacy, phonological patterning and phonetic realization pull in different directions. It is the closest published statement of the position [ties.md](ties.md) and [distance.md](distance.md) take, and worth reading before deciding any feature table is wrong.
- Hayes. 1998. "Some Research Strategies for Feature Theory." Handout, UC Berkeley. <https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesSomeResearchStrategiesForFeatureTheory1998.pdf>. **Author copy.** A short, teachable statement of how one would decide between feature theories rather than assert one.
- Flemming. 2005. "Deriving Natural Classes in Phonology." *Lingua* 115, prepublication version. <http://web.mit.edu/flemming/www/paper/natclass-prepub.pdf>. **Author copy.** Natural classes derived from phonetic similarity rather than stipulated by a feature inventory. This is the most direct challenge in the list to the premise that a declared feature bundle is the primitive; his 2006 manuscript on distinctiveness constraints, at the same site, extends it to inventories.
- Hayes & Steriade. 2004. "Introduction: The phonetic bases of phonological markedness." In *Phonetically Based Phonology*. <https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndSteriade2004IntroToPBP.pdf>. **Author copy.** The framing chapter for phonetically grounded phonology, and the accessible way into everything below it. Hayes 1999, "Phonetically Driven Phonology," on the same site, is the longer argument.
- Steriade. 2001/2008. "The Phonology of Perceptibility Effects: the P-map and its consequences for constraint organization." <http://lingphil.mit.edu/papers/steriade/Steriade2001P-map.pdf>. **Author copy.** Perceptual similarity between segments as a structured object that predicts which repair a language chooses. It is the strongest published statement of what a segment-distance metric is *for*, and reading it will change what you think [distance.md](distance.md) should be measured against. Her 1997 laryngeal-neutralization manuscript and 2001 place-assimilation paper, at the same site, are the case studies; both are image scans with no text layer.
- Frisch, Pierrehumbert & Broe. 2004. "Similarity Avoidance and the OCP." *NLLT* 22(1), 179–228. <https://www.phon.ox.ac.uk/jpierrehumbert/publications/similarity_OCP.pdf>. **Author copy.** Similarity computed as shared natural classes over total classes, fitted against Arabic root cooccurrence. It is the closest published analogue to what [distance.md](distance.md) computes, it is derived from a different premise, and the disagreement is the interesting part. Pierrehumbert's 1993 "Dissimilarity in the Arabic Verbal Roots," at the same site, is the shorter precursor.
- Hayes & Wilson. 2008. "A Maximum Entropy Model of Phonotactics and Phonotactic Learning." *Linguistic Inquiry* 39(3), 379–440. <https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndWilsonPhonotactics2008.pdf>. **Author copy.** A learner that induces weighted constraints over feature-defined natural classes from a lexicon, with the software free and runnable at <https://www.brucehayes.org/Phonotactics/>. For anyone with a machine-learning background this is the most direct bridge into phonological theory, because the model is one they already know. The manual is the practical half. Note the extraction trap below before quoting any formula.
- Hayes & White. 2013. "Phonological Naturalness and Phonotactic Learning." *Linguistic Inquiry* 44(1), 45–75. <https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/HayesWhite2013PhonologicalNaturalnessAndPhonotacticLearning.pdf>. **Author copy.** What the learner above gets wrong, and the argument that learners are biased toward phonetically natural generalizations. Read the two together or neither.
- Daland, Hayes, White, Garellek, Davis & Norrmann. 2011. "Explaining sonority projection effects." *Phonology* 28(2). <https://www.brucehayes.org/papers/DalandHayesEtAl2011ExplainingSonorityProjection.pdf>. **Author copy.** Several models of sonority tested against speaker judgments on unattested clusters. Read it before treating any sonority scale — including a derived one — as a fact rather than a model.
- Gorman. 2013. *Generative Phonotactics*. PhD dissertation, University of Pennsylvania. <https://www.wellformedness.com/papers/gorman-dissertation.pdf>. **Author copy.** The skeptical case: how much of what phonotactic grammars are credited with explaining is lexical statistics. His 2011 "A program for phonotactic theory," at the same site, is the short version.
- Hume. 2001. "Metathesis: Formal and Functional Considerations." **Author copy**, and Buckley. 2011. "Metathesis." Ch. 59 of *The Blackwell Companion to Phonology*, <https://www.ling.upenn.edu/~gene/papers/Buckley2011_metathesis.pdf>, **author copy**. The two surveys of the process that segment-rewriting notations cannot state. Buckley is the shorter orientation; Hume carries the functional account. Both bear on [design/captures.md](design/captures.md).
- Selkirk. 1995/96. "The Prosodic Structure of Function Words." *UMass Occasional Papers in Linguistics* 18. <http://people.umass.edu/selkirk/pdf/PSFWUMOP'%20copy.pdf>. **Author copy.** Where the morphological seam and the prosodic word come apart, which is the question [design/morph-boundary.md](design/morph-boundary.md) answers by giving the morph its own tier. A truncated six-page extract circulates; the full paper runs to 53 pages, and the link above is the full one.

## 3. Phonetics, the IPA and articulation

### The IPA's own documents, and IPA as text

- International Phonetic Association. *The International Phonetic Alphabet*, current Kiel edition. <https://www.internationalphoneticassociation.org/IPAcharts/common_files/pdfs/pdfs_IPA_charts_E/IPA_Kiel.pdf>. **Open access, CC BY-SA 4.0**, with the reproduction terms stated at <https://www.internationalphoneticassociation.org/content/ipa-chart>. The chart is licensed for reproduction, which is what makes it assignable and reusable in teaching material. The 2020 revision and the 2005 remastered edition are archived at the same site and are worth having when a citation names a year. **Do not extract text from any of these PDFs** — see the traps section.
- International Phonetic Association. *IPA Number Chart*. <https://www.internationalphoneticassociation.org/IPAcharts/common_files/pdfs/pdfs_IPA_charts_E/IPA_number_chart.pdf>. **Open access.** The stable numeric identifiers for every symbol, which is the right key to join on when the glyph itself is what is in dispute. Between this and a Unicode correspondence table you can reconstruct the whole inventory without trusting a font.
- Wells et al. IPA and Unicode symbol correspondence table. <https://speechandhearing.net/resource/phonetics/ipa-unicode.htm>. **Open access.** Symbol to code point, in a form you can read and check by hand.
- Moran & Cysouw. 2018. *The Unicode Cookbook for Linguists: Managing Writing Systems Using Orthography Profiles*. Language Science Press. <https://langsci-press.org/catalog/book/176>. **Open access, CC BY.** The book to read before writing any code that handles IPA as text: normalization forms, combining-mark order, the difference between a grapheme and a code point, and orthography profiles as the discipline that keeps a corpus honest. Every failure [design/ecosystem.md](design/ecosystem.md) records at the string boundary is a failure this book warns about.
- Unicode Consortium. *Unicode Security Mechanisms* (UTS #39) confusables data. <https://www.unicode.org/Public/security/latest/confusables.txt>. **Open access.** The authoritative list of characters that look alike, which is the reference for a lookalike policy rather than a hand-built substitution table. Grep it by code point; grepping by glyph is how you get the wrong answer from a file about glyphs.
- Miller, Fedotov & Vydrin. 2025. *Unicode request for extended IPA tone diacritics*, L2/25-250. <https://unicode.org/L2/L2025/25250-ipa-tone-diacritics.pdf>. **Open access.** The proposal document that gives the readings of the four extended tone diacritics, cited by [tone.md](tone.md). Also a good short example of how a phonetic character actually gets standardized, which is not obvious from the outside.

### Phonetics, and what a class can be given

- Anderson et al. (eds.). 2022. *Essentials of Linguistics*, 2nd edition. eCampusOntario Pressbooks. <https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/essentialsoflinguistics2/>. **Open access, CC BY-SA.** A complete, openly licensed introductory text with substantial phonetics and phonology chapters. It is the answer to what a class can actually be assigned, and it is the only entry in this list that is both complete and freely relicensable. Chapters 4.6 and 4.8 cover rule interaction and bleeding order.
- Ladefoged. *A Course in Phonetics* supplementary web materials, <http://www.phonetics.ucla.edu/course/contents.html>, and the *Vowels and Consonants* language index, <https://phonetics.ucla.edu/index/language.html>. **Open access, CC BY-SA 3.0.** The audio and the language samples, free, even though the textbooks they accompany are not. For teaching, the recordings are most of what the book was for.
- UCLA Phonetics Lab Archive. <http://archive.phonetics.ucla.edu/main_frameset_files/intro.htm>. **Open access.** Field recordings and word lists for several hundred languages, collected under Ladefoged. Chodroff, Pažon, Baker & Moran. 2024. "Phonetic Segmentation of the UCLA Phonetics Lab Archive," *LREC-COLING 2024*, <https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.19509>, **open access**, is the forced-aligned version and the paper to read before using the archive as training data.

### Articulatory phonology and task dynamics

- Browman & Goldstein. 1992. "Articulatory Phonology: An Overview." *Phonetica* 49, 155–180. **Open access** through the Haskins status-report archive. The entry point to the area, and the single most directly relevant page in it for this repository is Figure 1, the inventory of tract variables and the articulators that realize them — lip protrusion and aperture, tongue tip and tongue body constriction location and degree, velic aperture, glottal aperture. That is the model [tract-anatomy.md](tract-anatomy.md) declares, arrived at independently. Note it is a figure, not a table, so it will not be found by searching the text.
- Browman & Goldstein. 1989. "Articulatory gestures as phonological units." *Phonology* 6, 201–251, and 1990. "Tiers in articulatory phonology, with some implications for casual speech." In *Papers in Laboratory Phonology I*. **Open access**, same archive. The argument that the gesture, not the feature bundle, is the phonological primitive, and the 1990 paper is the tier architecture [design/tiers.md](design/tiers.md) adopts the shape of. Their 1986 "Towards an articulatory phonology" is the founding paper and is a page-image scan with no text layer.
- Saltzman & Munhall. 1989. "A dynamical approach to gestural patterning in speech production." *Ecological Psychology* 1, 333–382. **Open access**, same archive. Task dynamics: gestures as dynamical systems over tract variables, coordinated rather than sequenced. This is the machinery under everything in the previous entry, and it is where the coordination of gestures stops being a metaphor. Saltzman's single-authored 1986 preliminary model is the precursor, is a scan with no text layer, and is frequently miscited — see the citations section.
- Saltzman, Nam, Krivokapić & Goldstein. 2008. "A task-dynamic toolkit for modeling the effects of prosodic structure on articulation." *Speech Prosody 2008*. <https://www.isca-archive.org/speechprosody_2008/saltzman08_speechprosody.pdf>. **Open access.** Prosodic structure as a gesture that slows the clock at a boundary, rather than as a symbol inserted into a string. For anyone working on prosody in a TTS front end this reframes the problem usefully. Byrd, Lee & Campos-Astorkiza. 2008, "Phrase boundary effects on the temporal kinematics of sequential tongue tip consonants," *JASA* 123(6), <https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC2527637>, **open access**, is the measurement; Parrell, Lee & Byrd. 2010, "Evaluation of juncture strength using articulatory synthesis of prosodic gestures," *Speech Prosody 2010*, **open access**, is the synthesis test.
- Nam, Mitra, Tiede, Hasegawa-Johnson, Espy-Wilson, Saltzman & Goldstein. 2012. "A procedure for estimating gestural scores from speech acoustics." *JASA* 132(6), 3980–3989. <https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC3528686>. **Open access.** Recovering a gestural score from the signal, which is what makes articulatory phonology testable at scale rather than by hand-annotated example.
- Parrell, Ramanarayanan, Nagarajan & Houde. 2019. "The FACTS model of speech motor control." *PLoS Computational Biology* 15(9). <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1007321>. **Open access.** Task dynamics under state feedback control — the current form of the model, with the control theory made explicit. The best single modern statement if you are coming from engineering rather than from phonology.
- Kim, Erickson, Lee & Narayanan. "A study of invariant properties and variation patterns in the Converter/Distributor model for emotional speech." **No access restriction on the conference paper.** The C/D model treats the syllable as the atom and has no segment at all, which makes it the sharpest available contrast with a segment-spine representation, and the reason it is filed against the tier work in this repository rather than adopted. Fujimura's own presentation of the model in the AMS DIMACS series volume, 1994, is **not open access** — the copy consulted is a personally licensed purchase that cannot be redistributed, and the record held for it does not carry the chapter title, so it is cited here without one rather than with an invented one.

### Vocal tract models and synthesizers

- Thapen. 2017. *Pink Trombone*. <https://dood.al/pinktrombone/>. **Open access**, MIT-licensed source, self-contained in one page. An interactive vocal tract you can drag with a mouse and hear. For teaching, it does in thirty seconds what a chapter cannot, and the source is short enough to read as a specification of a simple articulatory synthesizer.
- Haskins Laboratories. ASY articulatory synthesizer demonstration. <https://haskinslaboratories.org/asy-demo>. **Open access.** The surviving public record of the Mermelstein articulatory model, whose 1973 paper is paywalled at the publisher and is not in the Haskins archive. Read it as documentation of the model rather than as a paper.
- Birkholz. 2005. *3D-Artikulatorische Sprachsynthese*. Dissertation, Universität Rostock. <https://www.vocaltractlab.de/publications/birkholz-2005-dissertation.pdf>. **Open access.** The actual specification of the VocalTractLab geometric vocal tract model, and the closest openly documented model to the geometry [tract-anatomy.md](tract-anatomy.md) describes. It is in German, which is the only reason it is not the first recommendation in this subsection. The project self-hosts its whole publication list free at <https://www.vocaltractlab.de/>.
- Birkholz. 2013. "Modeling consonant-vowel coarticulation for articulatory speech synthesis." *PLoS ONE* 8(4). **Open access.** The coarticulation model in English and in a journal anyone can reach, which makes it the practical entry to the previous entry.
- Birkholz et al. 2020. "Printable 3D vocal tract shapes from MRI data and their acoustic and aerodynamic properties." *Scientific Data* 7, 255. **Open access.** The Dresden Vocal Tract Dataset: MRI-derived shapes released as printable geometry, with measured acoustics. A rare case of an articulatory model you can hold.
- Story, Titze & Hoffman. 1996. "Vocal tract area functions from magnetic resonance imaging." *JASA* 100(1). **Author copy**; not open at the publisher. The canonical MRI-derived area-function reference, and the source [design/tract-validation.md](design/tract-validation.md) checks the declared place arcs against. Read it to understand what an area function can and cannot tell you about where a constriction is — the paper itself says the narrowing at 4–5 cm is the piriform sinuses rather than a lingual constriction, which is the fact that sinks the vowel half of that assessment.
- Story. 2018. "An age-dependent vocal tract model for males and females based on anatomic measurements." *JASA* 143. <https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC5966313>. **Open access.** The same line of work, open, and parameterized by age and sex — which matters for anyone whose synthetic voices are not all adult male.
- Gaines, Kim, Parrell, Houde et al. 2021. "Discrete constriction locations describe a comprehensive range of vocal tract shapes in the Maeda model." *JASA Express Letters*. **Author copy**; not open at the publisher. Sample a continuous articulatory model comprehensively and the tongue-body constriction locations come out *discrete*. That is an external result for the premise this repository's tract model is built on, reached from the opposite direction — the library derives discrete constrictions from declared features, this derives them from a parametric model — and it is one of the two sources that say the back vowels use two tongue-body constriction locations where [tract-anatomy.md](tract-anatomy.md) has one.

### Acoustic-to-articulatory inversion and articulatory representations

Newer, almost entirely on arXiv, and the fastest-moving part of this list.

- Cho et al. 2024. "Coding Speech through Vocal Tract Kinematics" (SPARC). **Open access**, arXiv. The entry point: an articulatory codec that encodes speech into interpretable kinematic trajectories and resynthesizes from them. Its twelve EMA dimensions are what [design/ecosystem.md](design/ecosystem.md) reads as the field's working articulatory interface, and the open request on its tracker for a way to visualize that output as an animation is one reason this repository draws a tract at all.
- Wu, Chen, Cho, Watanabe, Goldstein, Black & Anumanchipalli. 2023. "Speaker-independent acoustic-to-articulatory speech inversion." *ICASSP 2023*, arXiv:2302.06774. **Open access.** Inversion that generalizes across speakers, which is the step that makes articulatory features usable as a representation rather than as a per-subject curiosity.
- Cho, Mohamed, Black & Anumanchipalli. 2023. "Self-supervised models of speech infer universal articulatory kinematics." arXiv:2310.10788. **Open access.** Self-supervised speech models turn out to encode articulatory structure that nobody put there. The most interesting result in this subsection for someone deciding whether an explicit articulatory model is still worth building.
- Beguš, Zhou, Wu & Anumanchipalli. 2022. "Articulation GAN: unsupervised modeling of articulatory learning." arXiv:2210.15173. **Open access.** Generating articulatory trajectories rather than spectra, learned without articulatory supervision.
- Anumanchipalli, Chartier & Chang. 2019. "Speech synthesis from neural decoding of spoken sentences." *Nature* 568, 493–498. **Open access** through Europe PMC, where the full text is served as JATS XML rather than as a PDF. Decoding an articulatory intermediate from cortical activity and synthesizing from it. Cited constantly and read less often than it should be; the articulatory intermediate is the reason it worked.
- Lian, Black, Lu, Goldstein, Watanabe & Anumanchipalli. 2022. "Articulatory representation learning via joint factor analysis and neural matrix factorization," arXiv:2210.16498, and "Deep neural convolutive matrix factorization for articulatory representation decomposition," arXiv:2204.00465. **Both open access.** The decomposition half of the same program, if the entries above leave you wanting the representation-learning detail. ## Traps in the copies themselves

Several of these works cannot be read the way a PDF is normally read, and one wrong assumption here costs a day.

- **Custom-encoded phonetic fonts defeat text extraction.** Hayes's *Introductory Phonology* and every IPA chart PDF are set in fonts whose text layer is not Unicode: extracting the chart yields nine real IPA code points, with `ɱ` coming out as `µ`, `ɸ β` as `F B`, `θ ð` as `T D`. Prose and page numbers extract correctly and everything phonetic does not, which is the dangerous shape of failure — the extraction looks fine. Read transcriptions and rule statements off rendered page images, and take the symbol inventory from the number chart plus a Unicode table.
- **Two extractions of the same file can disagree.** *SPE* extracted with different layout handling produces different text in places. Where they differ, the page is the authority.
- **Journal typesetting can map mathematics into unrelated code points.** *Linguistic Inquiry*'s rendering of Hayes & Wilson 2008 extracts `Σ` as a CJK glyph, `−` and `∈` and `=` likewise. The prose is clean; no formula should be quoted from an extraction of it.
- **Older scans have no text layer at all.** Koskenniemi 1983, Browman & Goldstein 1986, Saltzman 1986, and Steriade's 1997 and 2001 manuscripts are page images. They can be read and not searched.
- **OCR of a scan mangles exactly the characters a phonologist cares about.** In the Kenstowicz & Kisseberth OCR, arrows and slashes come through as ASCII approximations, so rule displays have to be read from the page even though the prose is reliable. Terlutte & Simplot's OCR keeps a French accent inside an English word.
- **A hyphenated line break hides a quotation from a grep.** A sentence spanning a line break in a two-column paper will not be found by searching for it as written. Whitespace-normalize before searching, and confirm a page number by locating the page marks around it rather than by trusting a reported number.

## Citations that are commonly wrong

- **There was no SIGMORPHON 2022 grapheme-to-phoneme shared task.** The multilingual g2p task ran in 2020 and 2021 only; the 2022 volume's shared tasks were morpheme segmentation and inflection.
- **The 2021 shared-task overview is cited as "Ashby et al." because its author list is alphabetical.** Its senior author sits sixth. Its title also says *Second*, not third.
- **Chandlee & Heinz 2012's PDF byline is in the opposite order from the anthology metadata.** The anthology order is the citable one; a reader opening the PDF should not conclude the citation is wrong.
- **"Task dynamic coordination of the speech articulators" is single-authored by Saltzman**, and is frequently cited as Saltzman & Kelso. It is dated 1986 although it appears in the October–December 1985 status report.
- **Two of the Berkeley articulatory papers are Lian first-author, not Wu**, despite the overlapping author lists.
- **McCawley's paper on the role of notation in generative phonology is 1973**, though at least one bibliography dates it 1972 and drops the leading word of the title, so a search on either year alone can miss it.
- **The widely circulated UCLA URL for Hayes & Wilson 2008 is dead.** The working path is on `brucehayes.org`, which serves the same content.
- **`mngu0.org` no longer belongs to the corpus** and now serves unrelated content, and the Haskins status-report archive is orphaned — `haskins.yale.edu` does not resolve, and the reports survive only in the Internet Archive with uneven coverage. Every Browman & Goldstein and Saltzman entry above comes from there.

A ResearchGate search surfaced only recent work *using* the model, one item of which — Gaines et al. 2021 — was taken and is listed above. Reachable in a browser by hand, and worth one attempt from a session that has one.
- **Story. 2005, the parametric area-function model, and Story & Titze 1998.** Both closed; the author's old institutional page is a 404 and its archived form holds unrelated files. Story 2018, open, was taken instead, and Story, Titze & Hoffman 1996 was found as an author deposit.
- **Byrd & Saltzman. 2003. "The elastic phrase,"** the original pi-gesture paper. Not open. A related 2006 paper is listed as open at PMC but both of that record's full-text endpoints error. Byrd et al. 2008 and Saltzman et al. 2008 were taken instead.
- **Bisani & Ney. 2008, the joint-sequence journal paper.** Closed, with zero open locations, and the authors' own publication server carries the entry with no download link. Their open 2003 conference paper, the direct precursor, is listed above.
- **Wilson & Gallagher. 2018. "Accidental Gaps and Surface-Based Phonotactic Learning."** Closed; three author and institutional pages checked, one 403, one a stale stub, one exposing no links outside JavaScript.
- **Parker, *Handouts for Advanced Phonology: A Course Packet*.** The document URL returns 403 to every user agent tried. It was reachable when [design/captures.md](design/captures.md) was written and that citation is not currently checkable.
- **Kim et al. 2014, the USC-EMO-MRI corpus paper, and Wrench 1999, the MOCHA corpus paper.** The first is closed; no reachable copy of the second's reference was found. The corpus documentation and Wrench & Richmond 2000 cover the same ground and are listed above.
- **TADA, the Task Dynamics Application.** A twenty-thousand-URL archive sweep of the whole hosting domain returns no hits. It is described in Saltzman et al. 2008 and Nam et al. 2012, both listed above.

Two retrieval traps are worth passing on to anyone assembling their own copies. Some repositories and publishers refuse a browser user agent and accept a bare `curl`, which is the reverse of the usual advice. And DSpace 7 repositories serve an empty application shell at the download path a page links to; the file itself is at a different API path, reachable from the item's bundle listing.

## What this list leaves out, and what it exposes

**Left out on purpose.** The issue trackers, forum threads and tool traces that [design/ecosystem.md](design/ecosystem.md) rests on are evidence, not reading — they answer what users complain about and they are not something to hand anyone. Works consulted during the assessments that decided nothing are also gone, on the same principle: a citation that supported no claim is a citation nobody needs to follow. Several papers were merged into a neighbor's entry where one annotation covered both, which is why some entries name three works. *Syntactic Structures* is not here: it is cited by one assessment for an appendix on notation, and it is not what anyone in the audiences above should read next. Neither is Eisner's 1997 paper on generation in primitive Optimality Theory, which is the primary result behind the two OT entries in §1 and is harder going than either; go to it if those leave you wanting the construction.

**Gaps this list exposes.** They are worth naming because they are also gaps in what this repository can currently be checked against.

- **Vocal-tract acoustic modeling is the thinnest area, and it is thin because it is closed.** Mermelstein 1973, Maeda's model papers and Story's 2005 parametric model are all paywalled, and they are the sources a geometric tract model would most want. What is open — Birkholz, Story 2018, Story, Titze & Hoffman 1996 as an author copy — is enough to work from and is not the canonical set.
- **Prosody and intonation have almost no representation here.** There is task-dynamic work on boundary effects and nothing at all on autosegmental-metrical intonational phonology, transcription systems for tone and intonation, or f0 modeling. For an audience that includes TTS front ends, that is the largest hole in the list.
- **Optimality Theory appears only through its computational treatments.** There is no primary statement of the theory and no textbook, because none that was checked is open.
- **Feature geometry has no primary source**, for the same reason: the standard handbook chapter is closed and its authors do not post it.
- **Speech perception and psycholinguistics are absent entirely.** The P-map is the only entry that argues from perception, and it argues from it rather than reporting it.
- **Descriptive grammars and language-particular phonologies are absent**, which is a real limit on a list meant partly for low-resource work: everything here is about method, and none of it is about a language.
